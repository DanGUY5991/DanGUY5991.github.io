01 / Doctoral direction

What makes an AI opportunity worthwhile?

Proposed research · Initial design stage

How do people work out whether AI makes sense for them—and who helps define a good decision?

My proposed doctoral research asks how Indigenous people develop understandings of AI through their ways of knowing, and how reciprocal learning can support community-defined decisions about entrepreneurial opportunities.

I want to understand how people learn about AI, whose advice they trust, and how they weigh possible benefits alongside responsibilities to others. A worthwhile outcome may be to adopt, adapt, postpone or decline a technology.

As a learner, I am interested in Leroy Little Bear’s work on Indigenous and Western knowledge and in Two-Eyed Seeing, associated with Mi’kmaw Elder Albert Marshall. I want to explore what these approaches might offer while respecting the differences between knowledge traditions.

Further reading: Leroy Little Bear’s work · Two-Eyed Seeing and Albert Marshall.

Working title
Indigenous Epistemologies and Decision-Making: Negotiating the AI Entrepreneurial Opportunity and Community Accountability

This direction informs my SSHRC Canada Graduate Research Scholarship—Doctoral application. It describes proposed work, not a funded award or completed study. Doctoral partnerships, fieldwork arrangements and ethics approval remain to be developed.

  1. Knowledge & trust

    What counts as useful knowledge? How do experience, advice and relationships shape what people believe about AI?

  2. Opportunity & responsibility

    Where do technology providers' promises align with—or differ from—the priorities and responsibilities people identify for themselves?

  3. Learning & decisions over time

    How do understandings change through conversation and experience? What makes an opportunity worth pursuing, changing or declining?

How I intend to approach the work

Questions shaped together

I intend to develop questions, methods and useful outcomes with partners, respecting distinct First Nations, Inuit and Métis contexts and protocols.

Learning in both directions

I would contribute accessible explanations of AI while learning from participants about the knowledge, priorities and relationships that guide their decisions.

Accountability before access

Ethics approval, required community authorization and ongoing individual consent would precede recruitment. Agreements would guide how knowledge is held, interpreted and shared.

03 / Teaching & cases

More than words.
Stories for learning.

Writing for learning

I develop teaching cases that connect relevant organizational stories with course concepts. The work combines narrative with learning objectives, discussion guidance and assessment materials to support classroom design.

There is more to a story than its words. People’s experiences, relationships and the context in which a story is shared help give it meaning. In my case writing, I aim to listen for these layers and create space for students to question, reflect and learn.

I want to learn how Indigenous approaches to storytelling can deepen my understanding of stories as a way of learning and growing. This interest connects my case work with my doctoral inquiry, while respecting the distinct people, communities and traditions from which stories come.

04 / Background

A practical path
into research.

I am Daniel Pearson, a doctoral student in Cultural, Social, and Political Thought at the University of Lethbridge. My work connects questions about knowledge and interpretation with the everyday decisions people make around technology.

My background includes restaurant management, staff development, municipal bylaw enforcement and dispatch. University research and teaching support have added experience in case writing, class preparation, assignment design and marking.

I am also learning about and reconnecting with my Indigenous heritage. This personal learning informs my interests in knowledge and relationships, alongside a commitment to respecting the authority of people and communities to speak for themselves.
